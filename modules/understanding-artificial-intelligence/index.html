Learning Journey

Unit Learning

Selected unit learning is documented here where it contributes meaningfully to my academic, technical, or professional development. The portfolio focuses on my own learning and reflection rather than reproducing University learning materials.

Unit 1

Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

This unit established the foundations for examining Artificial Intelligence as both a technical discipline and an organisational capability.

I explored how AI can be defined, how the field has developed, and why successful application depends on more than the technical system itself.

Key learning: AI implementation needs to be considered in relation to the problem being solved, the available data, organisational readiness, governance, and the wider context in which the system operates.

Unit 2

AI, Ethics and Responsible Adoption

This unit developed my understanding of the relationship between the potential benefits of Artificial Intelligence and the legal, ethical, social, and organisational considerations surrounding its use.

A particularly important development in my thinking was that business value and responsible AI cannot be assessed separately. An AI system may provide significant operational or commercial benefits while simultaneously creating risks relating to bias, fairness, transparency, privacy, or accountability.

Key learning: governance should develop alongside an AI solution throughout its lifecycle rather than being treated primarily as a compliance activity at the end of development.

Connection to CLD1: this perspective strengthened my argument that sustainable AI adoption requires organisations to combine investment in technology with proportionate governance and accountability.

Unit 3

Learning Systems and AI Quality

Unit 3 developed my thinking about what constitutes an effective or “good” AI system.

I initially tended to associate AI quality strongly with technical performance. My learning during this unit broadened that perspective. Performance remains important, but it is not sufficient on its own to determine whether an AI system is appropriate or successful.

The quality of an AI system also depends on factors such as the suitability of its data, its intended context of use, the consequences of incorrect outputs, the way people interact with its recommendations, and how its behaviour is evaluated over time.

Key learning: AI quality should be evaluated as a combination of technical performance, contextual suitability, responsible use, and continuing evaluation.

Connection to CLD1: this helped develop my final position that technology alone does not create sustainable AI value.

Collaborative Learning

Collaborative Learning Discussion 1

AI Adoption and Responsible Governance

Context

This collaborative discussion explored why organisations continue to invest heavily in Artificial Intelligence and what is required for AI adoption to generate sustainable value.

My initial contribution considered the increasing accessibility of AI through advances in computing, cloud technologies, and data availability, together with its potential to improve productivity, decision-making, and innovation.

The subsequent peer discussion and learning from Units 2 and 3 developed this position further, particularly in relation to governance, explainability, human oversight, and the wider criteria that should be used to evaluate an AI system.

Summary Post

From AI Investment to Responsible Adoption

My initial position was that AI has become ubiquitous because advances in computing, cloud technologies and data availability have made it increasingly accessible, while its potential to improve productivity, decision-making and innovation creates a strong incentive for organisational investment. The discussion and subsequent units reinforced this view but also developed my understanding of what constitutes successful AI adoption.

Unit 2 demonstrated that business benefits cannot be evaluated independently from ethical and regulatory implications. One peer contribution considered financial services, where AI can improve areas such as fraud detection and credit assessment but may also produce discriminatory outcomes. I agreed with the argument that governance must develop alongside AI investment. I would extend this further by arguing that proportionate governance should be integrated throughout the AI lifecycle rather than treated as a final compliance exercise.

The healthcare example in my initial contribution generated further discussion around explainability and human oversight. Peer responses highlighted both explainable AI and the risk of automation bias. These contributions challenged my assumption that retaining a human decision-maker necessarily provides sufficient oversight. If clinicians routinely accept algorithmic recommendations without critical evaluation, human involvement may become procedural rather than meaningful (Aubreville et al., 2024).

Unit 3 further developed this perspective by considering learning systems and what constitutes a good AI system. I now view technical performance as only one measure of quality. An effective AI system must also be appropriate to its context, supported by suitable data, and subject to continuing evaluation.

Overall, organisations have compelling reasons to invest in AI, but technology alone does not create sustainable value. Effective adoption requires organisations to combine technical capability with appropriate data, infrastructure, governance, transparency, and meaningful human accountability.

Personal Reflection

How My Thinking Developed

The most important development in my thinking was the distinction between having human involvement and having meaningful human oversight. I initially treated the presence of a human decision-maker as an important safeguard, but the discussion on automation bias showed that this safeguard can become ineffective if people defer routinely to an AI recommendation.

The discussion also reinforced my view that AI governance should not sit outside delivery as a final approval activity. Governance, data quality, explainability, monitoring, and accountability need to be considered throughout the lifecycle of an AI solution.

This is particularly relevant to my professional perspective because it connects technical AI performance with operating models, governance, adoption, and long-term organisational value.
