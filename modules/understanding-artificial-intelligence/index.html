Learning Journey

Unit Learning

Selected unit learning is documented here where it contributes meaningfully to my academic, technical, or professional development. The portfolio focuses on my own learning and reflection rather than reproducing University learning materials.

Unit 1

Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

This unit established the foundations for examining Artificial Intelligence as both a technical discipline and an organisational capability.

I explored how AI can be defined, how the field has developed, and why successful application depends on more than the technical system itself.

Key learning: AI implementation needs to be considered in relation to the problem being solved, the available data, organisational readiness, governance, and the wider context in which the system operates.

Unit 2

AI, Ethics and Responsible Adoption

This unit developed my understanding of the relationship between the potential benefits of Artificial Intelligence and the legal, ethical, social, and organisational considerations surrounding its use.

A particularly important development in my thinking was that business value and responsible AI cannot be assessed separately. An AI system may provide significant operational or commercial benefits while simultaneously creating risks relating to bias, fairness, transparency, privacy, or accountability.

Key learning: governance should develop alongside an AI solution throughout its lifecycle rather than being treated primarily as a compliance activity at the end of development.

Connection to CLD1: this perspective strengthened my argument that sustainable AI adoption requires organisations to combine investment in technology with proportionate governance and accountability.

Unit 3

Learning Systems and AI Quality

Unit 3 developed my thinking about what constitutes an effective or “good” AI system.

I initially tended to associate AI quality strongly with technical performance. My learning during this unit broadened that perspective. Performance remains important, but it is not sufficient on its own to determine whether an AI system is appropriate or successful.

The quality of an AI system also depends on factors such as the suitability of its data, its intended context of use, the consequences of incorrect outputs, the way people interact with its recommendations, and how its behaviour is evaluated over time.

Key learning: AI quality should be evaluated as a combination of technical performance, contextual suitability, responsible use, and continuing evaluation.

Connection to CLD1: this helped develop my final position that technology alone does not create sustainable AI value.

Collaborative Learning

Collaborative Learning Discussion 1

AI Adoption and Responsible Governance

Context

This collaborative discussion explored why organisations continue to invest heavily in Artificial Intelligence and what is required for AI adoption to generate sustainable value.

My initial contribution considered the increasing accessibility of AI through advances in computing, cloud technologies, and data availability, together with its potential to improve productivity, decision-making, and innovation.

The subsequent peer discussion and learning from Units 2 and 3 developed this position further, particularly in relation to governance, explainability, human oversight, and the wider criteria that should be used to evaluate an AI system.

Summary Post

From AI Investment to Responsible Adoption

My initial position was that AI has become ubiquitous because advances in computing, cloud technologies and data availability have made it increasingly accessible, while its potential to improve productivity, decision-making and innovation creates a strong incentive for organisational investment. The discussion and subsequent units reinforced this view but also developed my understanding of what constitutes successful AI adoption.

Unit 2 demonstrated that business benefits cannot be evaluated independently from ethical and regulatory implications. One peer contribution considered financial services, where AI can improve areas such as fraud detection and credit assessment but may also produce discriminatory outcomes. I agreed with the argument that governance must develop alongside AI investment. I would extend this further by arguing that proportionate governance should be integrated throughout the AI lifecycle rather than treated as a final compliance exercise.

The healthcare example in my initial contribution generated further discussion around explainability and human oversight. Peer responses highlighted both explainable AI and the risk of automation bias. These contributions challenged my assumption that retaining a human decision-maker necessarily provides sufficient oversight. If clinicians routinely accept algorithmic recommendations without critical evaluation, human involvement may become procedural rather than meaningful (Aubreville et al., 2024).

Unit 3 further developed this perspective by considering learning systems and what constitutes a good AI system. I now view technical performance as only one measure of quality. An effective AI system must also be appropriate to its context, supported by suitable data, and subject to continuing evaluation.

Overall, organisations have compelling reasons to invest in AI, but technology alone does not create sustainable value. Effective adoption requires organisations to combine technical capability with appropriate data, infrastructure, governance, transparency, and meaningful human accountability.

Personal Reflection

How My Thinking Developed

The most important development in my thinking was the distinction between having human involvement and having meaningful human oversight. I initially treated the presence of a human decision-maker as an important safeguard, but the discussion on automation bias showed that this safeguard can become ineffective if people defer routinely to an AI recommendation.

The discussion also reinforced my view that AI governance should not sit outside delivery as a final approval activity. Governance, data quality, explainability, monitoring, and accountability need to be considered throughout the lifecycle of an AI solution.

This is particularly relevant to my professional perspective because it connects technical AI performance with operating models, governance, adoption, and long-term organisational value.

Collaborative Learning

Collaborative Learning Discussion 2

Machine Learning Algorithm Selection and Evaluation

Context

This collaborative discussion explored the selection and evaluation of machine learning algorithms in relation to the business problem, the characteristics of the available data, and the wider decision context in which model outputs are used.

My initial contribution compared Random Forest and K-means as examples of supervised and unsupervised learning. I initially focused on matching algorithm choice to the nature of the problem and available data.

The peer discussion developed this position considerably by introducing questions around explainability, consequences of incorrect predictions, preprocessing, validation, causal interpretation, and the role of human judgement when acting on model outputs.

Submitted Summary Post

From Algorithm Selection to Decision Context

My initial position was that machine learning algorithm selection should primarily depend on the business problem and characteristics of the available data rather than technical sophistication. Comparing Random Forest with K-means supported this distinction between prediction using labelled data and pattern discovery from unlabelled data. However, the peer discussion broadened my understanding of what “context” should include when selecting and evaluating an algorithm.

Henry’s response highlighted that model selection should also consider the consequences of incorrect predictions. This developed my initial concern about Random Forest explainability: global feature importance may describe overall model behaviour without adequately explaining why an individual prediction was made. More importantly, subsequent discussion distinguished prediction from causal explanation. A model may accurately identify an order at risk of delay without demonstrating that prioritising the order will prevent that delay (Shmueli, 2010). I therefore now see model outputs as evidence supporting decisions rather than automatically determining interventions.

The discussion around K-means similarly challenged my initial focus on algorithm selection. Syed highlighted feature scaling and sensitivity to outliers, demonstrating how preprocessing decisions can substantially influence apparently objective clusters. Rachael extended this further by questioning whether discovered clusters are meaningful and sufficiently validated in practice. This reinforces that unsupervised learning requires interpretation and validation rather than assuming that mathematically identified groups represent meaningful real-world categories.

Overall, my understanding has shifted from selecting algorithms according to problem and data towards evaluating the complete decision context: data preparation, predictive performance, explainability, validation, consequences of error and human intervention. The discussion therefore reinforced that effective machine learning is not simply about selecting an algorithm, but about establishing whether its outputs are sufficiently reliable and meaningful to support the intended decision.

Assessment Outcome

Result and Tutor Feedback

Result: 70% — Distinction

The strongest aspect of the discussion was collaboration. The tutor highlighted my early participation, the questions I raised, and the way other participants developed arguments from my contributions.

My distinction between prediction and causal explanation, supported by Shmueli (2010), was identified as a particularly insightful development in the discussion. The tutor also positively highlighted my argument that unsupervised learning should not be considered inherently free from bias because human decisions still influence variable selection, scaling, preprocessing, and interpretation of clusters.

The principal area for improvement was criticality in the final Summary Post. Although the wider discussion contained stronger challenge and disagreement, my summary became comparatively agreeable and therefore did not fully demonstrate the critical position I had developed during the discussion.

The feedback also identified the need to strengthen the evidence base, ensure substantive claims are appropriately supported, audit the relationship between in-text citations and the reference list, and use UK English consistently.

Reflection Following Tutor Feedback

The Accuracy–Explainability Trade-off

One of my most important contributions during the discussion did not appear in my final Summary Post. In responding to a peer argument about explainability, I challenged the assumption that choosing a more interpretable model is automatically the safer option. If the more interpretable model performs substantially worse, the resulting reduction in predictive accuracy may itself introduce risk.

This exchange developed my understanding of the relationship between accuracy and explainability. I no longer view them as independent model characteristics where explainability should simply be maximised. Instead, the appropriate balance depends on the decision context, consequences of error, level of human oversight, regulatory requirements, and the purpose for which the model is being used.

This also connects to my wider learning from the discussion. Liu et al. (2023), for example, examine machine-learning-based population segmentation in clinical applications and demonstrate the importance of evaluating how analytical approaches translate into meaningful real-world use. Technical outputs require interpretation and validation within their application context.

The tutor feedback therefore highlighted an important development area for my academic writing: my strongest critical reasoning may emerge during discussion, but I need to ensure that this challenge, disagreement, and evaluation are carried through explicitly into my final written synthesis.

Development Actions

What I Will Apply to Future Work

Criticality: preserve the strongest points of challenge and disagreement from exploratory discussion when producing the final academic synthesis, rather than smoothing competing perspectives into consensus.

Evidence: support substantive analytical claims with a broader and more deliberate evidence base rather than relying on a small number of references.

Referencing: perform a final citation audit to confirm that every source in the reference list is used in-text and that claims requiring academic support are appropriately referenced.

Academic style: use UK English consistently throughout assessed work and portfolio reflections.
