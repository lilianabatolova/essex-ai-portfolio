Module Purpose

What I Am Developing

This module develops my understanding of Artificial Intelligence from foundational concepts through to the design, implementation and evaluation of learning systems.

My objective is not simply to understand how AI techniques work. I am developing the ability to evaluate whether an AI approach is appropriate for a particular problem, what data and infrastructure it requires, how performance should be assessed, and what governance and human oversight are necessary for responsible use.

This is particularly relevant to my professional development because it connects technical AI understanding with the wider decisions required to implement AI successfully in an enterprise environment.

Learning Journey

From Foundations to Evaluation

Rather than reproducing every weekly activity, this portfolio groups the module into the major stages that meaningfully changed or extended my understanding.

Units 1–3

AI Foundations & Responsible Adoption

The opening units established Artificial Intelligence as both a technical discipline and an organisational capability. I explored how AI has developed, why organisations invest in it, and how ethical, regulatory and social considerations affect its adoption.

A significant development in my thinking was recognising that business value and responsible AI cannot be evaluated separately. An AI system may deliver operational benefits while simultaneously creating risks involving bias, fairness, privacy, transparency or accountability.

I also moved beyond treating technical performance as the primary measure of AI quality. A good AI system must be appropriate to its context, supported by suitable data, evaluated continuously and accompanied by meaningful human accountability.

Key development: successful AI adoption requires technical capability, organisational readiness and proportionate governance to develop together.

Units 4–6

Developing Learning Systems

This stage moved from understanding AI conceptually towards understanding how learning systems are developed. I explored supervised and unsupervised learning, machine learning and deep learning, model training, and the importance of data in determining what a system can learn.

Practical activities introduced Jupyter Notebook, Scikit-learn and PyTorch as environments for implementing learning algorithms. This helped connect theoretical distinctions between learning approaches with the practical process of developing an AI solution.

Key development: algorithm selection is only one part of AI system design. Suitability also depends on the business problem, available data, training process, evaluation strategy and the decisions the resulting model is intended to support.

Units 7–9

Feature Engineering, Supervised Learning & Clustering

These units focused on the relationship between data preparation and model behaviour. I explored feature selection and representation, including how preprocessing and transformation decisions influence what a learning algorithm is able to identify.

These principles were then applied to supervised classification and regression and to unsupervised clustering. CRISP-DM provided a structured way of thinking about the development process: beginning with the problem and data, progressing through preparation and modelling, and evaluating whether the resulting system is appropriate for its intended purpose.

Key development: model outputs are not independent of the choices made before training. Feature selection, scaling, cleaning and representation can materially affect both supervised predictions and apparently objective clusters.

Unit 10

Evaluating Learning Systems

Evaluation extended my understanding beyond asking whether a model is simply “accurate”. I explored validation using unseen data and cross-validation together with measures such as accuracy, precision, recall, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score.

The key principle is that evaluation metrics must be interpreted in context. A model can perform well according to one metric while still being unsuitable for the decision it is intended to support, particularly where different types of error have different consequences.

The unit also introduced evaluation considerations for generative AI, reinforcing that different classes of learning systems require different approaches to assessing performance and quality.

Key development: I increasingly view model evaluation as a decision-design problem rather than a purely technical measurement exercise.

Collaborative Learning Discussion 1

AI Adoption and Responsible Governance

Context

This collaborative discussion explored why organisations continue to invest heavily in Artificial Intelligence and what is required for AI adoption to generate sustainable value.

My initial contribution considered the increasing accessibility of AI through advances in computing, cloud technologies and data availability, together with its potential to improve productivity, decision-making and innovation.

The subsequent discussion and learning from Units 2 and 3 developed this position further, particularly in relation to governance, explainability, human oversight and the wider criteria that should be used to evaluate an AI system.

Summary Post

From AI Investment to Responsible Adoption

My initial position was that AI has become ubiquitous because advances in computing, cloud technologies and data availability have made it increasingly accessible, while its potential to improve productivity, decision-making and innovation creates a strong incentive for organisational investment. The discussion and subsequent units reinforced this view but also developed my understanding of what constitutes successful AI adoption.

Unit 2 demonstrated that business benefits cannot be evaluated independently from ethical and regulatory implications. Discussion of financial services illustrated how AI can improve areas such as fraud detection and credit assessment while also creating the possibility of discriminatory outcomes. This strengthened my view that governance must develop alongside AI investment. I would extend this by arguing that proportionate governance should be integrated throughout the AI lifecycle rather than treated as a final compliance exercise.

The healthcare example in my initial contribution generated further discussion around explainability and human oversight. The discussion highlighted both explainable AI and the risk of automation bias. This challenged my assumption that retaining a human decision-maker necessarily provides sufficient oversight. If clinicians routinely accept algorithmic recommendations without critical evaluation, human involvement may become procedural rather than meaningful (Aubreville et al., 2024).

Unit 3 further developed this perspective by considering learning systems and what constitutes a good AI system. I now view technical performance as only one measure of quality. An effective AI system must also be appropriate to its context, supported by suitable data and subject to continuing evaluation.

Overall, organisations have compelling reasons to invest in AI, but technology alone does not create sustainable value. Effective adoption requires organisations to combine technical capability with appropriate data, infrastructure, governance, transparency and meaningful human accountability.

Personal Reflection

From Human Involvement to Meaningful Oversight

The most important development in my thinking was the distinction between having human involvement and having meaningful human oversight. I initially treated the presence of a human decision-maker as an important safeguard, but the discussion on automation bias showed that this safeguard can become ineffective if people routinely defer to an AI recommendation.

The discussion also reinforced my view that AI governance should not sit outside delivery as a final approval activity. Governance, data quality, explainability, monitoring and accountability need to be considered throughout the lifecycle of an AI solution.

This is particularly relevant to my professional perspective because it connects technical AI performance with operating models, governance, adoption and long-term organisational value.

Collaborative Learning Discussion 2 · 70% Distinction

Algorithm Selection and Evaluation

Context

This collaborative discussion explored the selection and evaluation of machine learning algorithms in relation to the business problem, the characteristics of the available data and the wider decision context in which model outputs are used.

My initial contribution compared Random Forest and K-means as examples of supervised and unsupervised learning. I initially focused on matching algorithm choice to the nature of the problem and available data.

The discussion developed this position considerably by introducing questions around explainability, consequences of incorrect predictions, preprocessing, validation, causal interpretation and the role of human judgement when acting on model outputs.

Submitted Summary Post

From Algorithm Selection to Decision Context

My initial position was that machine learning algorithm selection should primarily depend on the business problem and characteristics of the available data rather than technical sophistication. Comparing Random Forest with K-means supported this distinction between prediction using labelled data and pattern discovery from unlabelled data. However, the peer discussion broadened my understanding of what “context” should include when selecting and evaluating an algorithm.

One response highlighted that model selection should also consider the consequences of incorrect predictions. This developed my initial concern about Random Forest explainability: global feature importance may describe overall model behaviour without adequately explaining why an individual prediction was made. More importantly, subsequent discussion distinguished prediction from causal explanation. A model may accurately identify an order at risk of delay without demonstrating that prioritising the order will prevent that delay (Shmueli, 2010). I therefore now see model outputs as evidence supporting decisions rather than automatically determining interventions.

Discussion around K-means similarly challenged my initial focus on algorithm selection. Feature scaling and sensitivity to outliers demonstrated how preprocessing decisions can substantially influence apparently objective clusters. The discussion also questioned whether discovered clusters are meaningful and sufficiently validated in practice. This reinforces that unsupervised learning requires interpretation and validation rather than assuming that mathematically identified groups represent meaningful real-world categories.

Overall, my understanding shifted from selecting algorithms according to problem and data towards evaluating the complete decision context: data preparation, predictive performance, explainability, validation, consequences of error and human intervention. Effective machine learning is therefore not simply about selecting an algorithm, but about establishing whether its outputs are sufficiently reliable and meaningful to support the intended decision.

Portfolio note: The text above represents my submitted Summary Post. It is intentionally retained separately from the reflection below so that subsequent learning following tutor feedback is clearly distinguishable from the assessed submission.

Assessment Outcome

Result and Tutor Feedback

Result: 70% — Distinction

Collaboration was the strongest aspect of the discussion. Tutor feedback highlighted my early participation, the questions I raised and the way other participants developed arguments from my contributions.

My distinction between prediction and causal explanation, supported by Shmueli (2010), was identified as a particularly insightful development. The feedback also positively highlighted my argument that unsupervised learning should not be considered inherently free from bias because human decisions still influence variable selection, scaling, preprocessing and interpretation of clusters.

The principal area for improvement was criticality in the final Summary Post. Although the wider discussion contained stronger challenge and disagreement, my summary became comparatively agreeable and therefore did not fully demonstrate the critical position I had developed.

The feedback also identified the need to strengthen the evidence base, ensure substantive claims are appropriately supported, audit the relationship between in-text citations and the reference list, and use UK English consistently.

Reflection Following Tutor Feedback

The Accuracy–Explainability Trade-off

One of my most important contributions during the discussion did not appear in my final Summary Post. In responding to an argument about explainability, I challenged the assumption that choosing a more interpretable model is automatically the safer option. If the more interpretable model performs substantially worse, the resulting reduction in predictive accuracy may itself introduce risk.

This exchange developed my understanding of the relationship between accuracy and explainability. I no longer view them as independent model characteristics where explainability should simply be maximised. Instead, the appropriate balance depends on the decision context, consequences of error, level of human oversight, regulatory requirements and the purpose for which the model is being used.

This connects to my wider learning from the discussion. Liu et al. (2023) examine machine-learning-based population segmentation in clinical applications and demonstrate the importance of evaluating how analytical approaches translate into meaningful real-world use. Technical outputs require interpretation and validation within their application context.

The tutor feedback therefore highlighted an important development area for my academic writing: my strongest critical reasoning may emerge during discussion, but I need to ensure that challenge, disagreement and evaluation are carried explicitly into my final written synthesis.

Development Actions

What I Will Apply to Future Work

Criticality: preserve the strongest points of challenge and disagreement from exploratory discussion when producing the final academic synthesis rather than smoothing competing perspectives into consensus.

Evidence: support substantive analytical claims with a broader and more deliberate evidence base.

Referencing: perform a final citation audit to confirm that every source in the reference list is used in-text and that claims requiring academic support are appropriately referenced.

Academic style: use UK English consistently throughout assessed work and portfolio reflections.

Summative Assessment · 40% · 68% Merit

Artificial Intelligence and Its Applications

From Machine Learning to Large Language Models

Focus: Evaluating AI Technologies for Pharmaceutical Wholesale and Distribution

This management-oriented report compared traditional machine learning, deep learning and large language model frameworks and evaluated their suitability for different problems within pharmaceutical distribution.

Central argument: increasing technological sophistication does not automatically create greater business value. ML, DL and LLMs should be treated as complementary technologies, with organisations selecting the least complex approach capable of delivering the required performance, business value and level of governance.

The analysis considered differences in data requirements, feature engineering and representation learning, computational requirements, explainability, risk and potential applications.

Assessment Feedback

What Worked Well

Tutor feedback highlighted the conceptual clarity of the comparison between ML, DL and LLMs and recognised the central thesis as coherent and consistently maintained. The pharmaceutical-distribution context was considered a useful business focus supported by relevant sector-specific evidence.

The report was particularly successful in communicating technical concepts to a management audience. Its structure and accessible writing were identified as strengths.

The principal development areas were to test the central argument more actively with evidence, deepen the technical explanation of training and model adaptation, broaden the evidence base, and progress the final recommendation from a portfolio of technologies towards a prioritised investment plan.

Assessment result: 68% — Merit

Reflection Following Tutor Feedback

From Technology Comparison to Investment Decision

The feedback reinforced that having a clear argument is not sufficient on its own: a strong academic argument must also be actively tested. Although I consistently argued that organisations should avoid unnecessary model complexity, I repeated this principle more often than I challenged it with comparative evidence or considered circumstances in which a more complex approach might be justified.

I also identified a gap between understanding AI technologies conceptually and explaining their technical implementation in sufficient depth. A stronger version of the report would examine neural-network training more explicitly and distinguish between approaches to adapting and using LLMs, including pre-training, fine-tuning, instruction tuning, prompting, reinforcement learning from human feedback and retrieval-augmented generation.

The most important professional learning concerns the recommendation itself. A portfolio of possible AI technologies is not yet an investment strategy. For management, I should have progressed one step further by prioritising an initial use case and defining its required data, expected value, success measures, indicative cost and implementation sequence.

This distinction is directly relevant to my professional work in AI delivery. I increasingly see effective AI leadership not as identifying where advanced technology can be applied, but as establishing where AI creates sufficient incremental value to justify its complexity, cost and risk — and translating that judgement into an executable investment decision.
